# The Competitive Manifesto

*Why this candidacy, against the field it actually competes with. Written to be
believed by the person reading it, including me, the morning of.*

---

## The field

Be honest about who else is in the folder: computer-science students with stronger
algorithmic training; candidates with prior internships; a stack of AI-flavored
portfolio projects — sentiment analyzers, stock predictors, chatbots — most of them
tutorials wearing a haircut. And a few genuinely strong builders.

This manifesto is not "I'm better than them." It's: **on the three axes this specific
team decides on, here is where the position is unassailable, and here is where it
isn't — and what to do about each.**

## Axis 1: Evidence density — UNASSAILABLE

Every candidate asserts. This candidacy *demonstrates*: a working FCC product mapped
line-by-line to the JD, 82 CI-enforced tests, honest held-out metrics, a security
surface with an adversarial corpus, a self-authored code review, and a presentation
package that performs the JD's own communication requirement in real time.

The structural advantage: **assertions scale infinitely, evidence doesn't.** Nobody
can fake a hash-chained investigator in an interview. The strategy is to keep every
conversation anchored to artifacts — page numbers, test names, metrics — because on
the evidence axis, this candidacy doesn't compete; it referees.

## Axis 2: Domain alignment — UNASSAILABLE

Generic AI projects optimize for impressiveness. Verita optimizes for *this
industry's fear*: indefensible decisions. The thesis — every number shows its work —
is not a personal aesthetic; it's Wolters Kluwer's market position rendered as
software. No competing candidate built for FCC's actual constraint, because almost
nobody outside the industry knows what the constraint is.

The strategy: never let the project be categorized as "another AI dashboard." The
category is "auditable AI" — and in that category, the field is empty.

## Axis 3: Raw engineering pedigree — CONCEDED, AND REFRAMED

A CS senior with three internships out-credentials this candidacy on paper, and
pretending otherwise would violate the project's own honesty policy. Concede it
fast, every time, with a smile — and then reframe what an internship buys:

**Internships purchase slope, not intercepts.** The intercept (current production
experience) is conceded. The slope — zero to shipped FCC product in weeks, the
documented learning velocity, the AI-native workflow with verification discipline —
is the steepest thing in the folder. Teams that hire intercepts get what they
measured; teams that hire slopes get compound interest.

## The deepest differentiator (say it only if the moment is right)

Every company is about to spend a decade learning how to adopt AI without losing
trust. The candidates who matter for that decade aren't the ones who *use* AI most —
they're the ones who've already built the **verification habits**: review gates,
provenance records, adversarial tests, honest disclosure. This candidacy arrives with
those habits installed and *documented* — the AI-transparency page isn't interview
defense, it's a working prototype of the workflow their teams will need.

Most candidates bring skills. A few bring evidence. This one brings a *method* —
and the method is the thing that survives every technology cycle the next decade
throws at it.

## The manifesto, in one paragraph

I can't out-credential the field, so I out-evidenced it. I can't claim their
experience, so I brought my slope, measured. I aligned with the one constraint this
industry cannot trade away — defensibility — and built it into everything: the
product, the metrics, the documents, and the way I'll answer questions today.
**Others promised. I shipped — for this team, specifically.** That sentence is
either worth an internship or it isn't, and I'm content to let the evidence decide,
because betting on evidence is the entire point of the project, the candidacy,
and — if they hire me — the work.
