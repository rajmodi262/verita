# What To Do With Your Hands

*Your hands either reinforce your credibility or destroy it. There is no neutral.*

---

## Section 1: When to USE your hands — architecture explanations

Systems live in space; let them. When explaining the architecture:

- Left hand becomes the frontend, right hand the backend, the gap between them the
  API. Move data across the gap with a small gesture, not a throw.
- Stack layers top-to-bottom (UI high, database low) — match the blueprint's
  geography so your hands and your printed diagrams agree with each other.
- Draw the hash chain as small linked loops, left to right, one per step. When you
  say "doctor one step and the chain breaks," *snap the gesture once*. That snap is
  worth a paragraph.
- Keep the canvas small — chest height, shoulder width. Conducting Beethoven reads
  as nerves; sketching on an invisible whiteboard reads as mastery.

## Section 2: When to STILL your hands — the human moments

Failure story, weaknesses, the integrity answer, anything from Chapter B of the HR
file: **hands on the table, loosely clasped or flat.** Stillness during vulnerable
answers signals "nothing here is performed." Moving hands during a confession reads
as selling it; selling a confession voids it.

## Section 3: The prop move — picking up the bundle

Once or twice per interview, maximum, and only at a genuine peak:

1. Pick up YOUR copy. Turn to the page. Place it facing them.
2. Point at the specific element — the dim star, the iceberg's depth line, the
   AI Annex. One finger, one element.
3. Then *lean back*. The document is now presenting; you are its colleague, not
   its narrator.

The prop move says: my evidence is physical, and I know exactly where everything
lives in it. Fumbling for a page costs more than the move earns — which is why you
rehearse the page numbers (poster 1, comic 2, transparency 6, iceberg 8).

## Section 4: The lean

- **Forward ten degrees**: when THEY are talking, when asking your closing
  question, during HR connection moments. The lean is listening made visible.
- **Back to neutral**: when delivering technical authority. Authority sits
  upright; eagerness leans. Use the difference deliberately.
- Never lean back with hands behind head. You know this. Under stress, bodies
  forget. Now it's written down.

## Section 5: The pause — how to physically hold one

A pause collapses if your body fidgets through it. To hold two seconds:

- Eyes stay on the asker (drop to the table and the pause reads as floundering;
  hold eye contact and the *same two seconds* read as thinking).
- Hands still. One slow nod is permitted — it says "good question" without
  spending words.
- Then answer slightly *slower* than your instinct. Speed after a pause betrays
  that the pause was theater.

If you need longer: "That's a good question — give me a second to answer it
properly." Ten more seconds, purchased, premium rate, worth it.

---

## THE ONE THING that kills technical presentations

**Looking at your own materials while speaking.**

Know the bundle so well you can cite page numbers while holding eye contact. Look
at the person; point at the page. The moment your eyes drop to your own document
mid-sentence, you've told the room the document is in charge.

You are in charge. The document is your evidence — and lawyers don't read their
evidence aloud while facing the exhibit. They face the jury.
