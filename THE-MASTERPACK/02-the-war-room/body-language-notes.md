# The Physical Performance

*A great answer delivered badly is a wasted answer. This file is about the 55% of the
interview that isn't words. Read it twice; rehearse it once in a mirror, feel
ridiculous, and then be grateful in the room.*

---

## THE ENTRANCE

Walk in at 70% speed. Not rushed — rushing says junior. Not slow — slow says
performance. Seventy percent.

Eyes go to the people, not the room. Smile at the first person whose eyes you meet.

**Place the bundle on the table before you sit.** Squarely, not tossed. Then sit.
**Do not explain it yet.** Let it sit there being a question they want to ask. The
bundle's first job is to be the most interesting object in the room for ninety
silent seconds.

Sit all the way back in the chair. Perched-on-the-edge reads as flight risk.

## THE HANDSHAKE MOMENT

When you hand over the printed bundles (after the opener, when the moment lands), the
exact words:

> "I made two versions — this one's the story, this one's the engineering.
> They're yours to keep either way."

Then the exact pause: **say nothing while they take them.** "Either way" is doing
quiet work — it says you're confident enough to decouple the gift from the outcome.
Do not narrate the contents. Do not say "so the first page is…" Let them open it.

## WHEN ANSWERING TECHNICAL QUESTIONS

- **Pause two full seconds before answering.** Count "one-Mississippi,
  two-Mississippi". The pause says the question deserved thought, and it buys your
  brain the framing time it actually needs. Fast answers read as rehearsed; you ARE
  rehearsed, so you must not read as it.
- **Draw the architecture in the air.** Left hand is the frontend, right hand is the
  backend, the space between them is the API. Hands that build invisible boxes while
  talking about systems make the systems feel real. Keep the boxes small and
  deliberate — conducting an orchestra is too much.
- **Reference the printed documents:** "Actually — I drew this exact thing; page 4
  of the tech bundle." Then turn YOUR copy to the page, not theirs. They'll turn
  theirs. Now you're two people reading a document together, which is a completely
  different room than interrogator-and-subject.
- **End deep answers with a return question:** "Does that match how you've seen it
  done here?" It converts examination into conversation, and their answer is free
  intelligence about the team.

## WHEN ANSWERING HR QUESTIONS

- Lean forward slightly. Ten degrees. HR questions are about connection; distance is
  for whiteboards.
- Use the interviewer's name once per three or four answers. More is salesmanship;
  zero is a missed string.
- **Smile on the RESULT beat of STAR answers.** You're reporting a win; let your face
  agree with your words. (Most candidates report their wins with funeral solemnity.)
- Hands still on the table for the vulnerable beats — the failure story, the
  weaknesses. Stillness reads as "I have nothing to hide."

## WHEN YOU DON'T KNOW THE ANSWER

NEVER the bare "I don't know" full-stop. NEVER a bluff — this team detects bluffs
professionally.

The script:

> "I haven't dealt with that specific case — my instinct from first principles would
> be [reason aloud, visibly thinking]. I'd want to verify that before acting on it.
> Is that something you run into often here?"

Reasoning aloud from first principles, delivered calmly, is worth MORE than a correct
answer delivered nervously. They are hiring the reasoning engine, not the lookup
table — the lookup table is what they have AI for.

## WHEN THEY LOOK AT YOUR PRINTED BUNDLE

**Go quiet. Let them read. Do not narrate.**

This is the hardest physical skill in the room and the one that signals the most
confidence. The documents were designed for exactly this moment — every page can
survive sixty seconds of silent scrutiny. Narrating over them says you don't trust
your own work. Watch which page holds them longest; mention nothing; use that
intelligence later ("you spent a while on the iceberg — want to go below the
waterline?").

## THE 45-MINUTE CHECK

Somewhere past the middle, energy sags — yours and theirs. The reset is physical,
not mental: sit up one vertebra taller, plant both feet, take one slow breath during
their next question. If water is on the table, drink during THEIR long questions,
never during your own answers.

## THE CLOSE — the last 60 seconds

When they signal the end (chairs shift, "well—", folders close):

1. **Ask your one question** (see `the-close.md`). Then actually listen. Nod at the
   content, not at your turn to speak.
2. Stand WITH them, not before. Push the chair in. Small physical courtesies are
   disproportionately remembered.
3. The exit line, with a real smile: *"Thank you for the sharpest questions my work
   has ever gotten. Whatever you decide — the one-pager's yours; everything on it is
   checkable."*
4. Handshake: firm, two seconds, eye contact, person's name.
5. **Leave at the same 70% speed you entered.** Do not look back through the glass.
   The interview ends when you're out of sight, not when you're out of the room.

## THE ONE THING THAT KILLS TECHNICAL PRESENTATIONS

**Looking at your own materials while speaking.**

Know the bundle so well that you can cite page numbers while holding eye contact.
Look at the person; point at the page. The moment your eyes drop to your own document
mid-sentence, you've told the room the document is in charge. You are in charge. The
document is your evidence, and lawyers don't read their evidence aloud facing the
exhibit — they face the jury.
